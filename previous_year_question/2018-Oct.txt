18. Pin diagram of a microprocessor given, name it with reasons.
The reasons, taken from the diagram:

Chip label: The package is marked "8086" in the center.
Package: It is a 40-pin DIP, with VCC on pin 40 and GND on pins 1 and 20.
16-bit multiplexed bus: Pins AD0 to AD15 (pins 2 to 16 and 39) carry the lower 16 address bits and the 16 data bits at different times. This shows a 16-bit data bus.
20-bit address bus: A16/S3 to A19/S6 (pins 35 to 38) extend the address to 20 bits, so it can address 2²⁰ = 1 MB of memory.
BHE'/S7 (pin 34): Bus High Enable selects the upper byte of the 16-bit bus, which is needed for byte or word access.
MN/MX' (pin 33): It selects minimum or maximum mode, a feature specific to the 8086/8088. The labels at the top right show the two functions of pins 24 to 31:
Minimum mode: HOLD, HLDA, WR', M/IO', DT/R', DEN', ALE, INTA'
Maximum mode: RQ'/GT0', RQ'/GT1', LOCK', S2', S1', S0', QS0, QS1. The queue status pins QS0 and QS1 show the instruction prefetch queue, which is characteristic of the 8086.
Other control and interrupt pins: NMI, INTR, TEST', CLK, READY, and RESET are all present.

25. What do you understand Business logic design? How will you do it?
Business Logic Design

Business logic is the set of business rules, conditions, calculations, validations, and processes that determine how an application operates according to the organization's requirements.

Example: In a sales system, calculating discount, VAT, stock availability, credit limit, and invoice amount are business logic.

How I would design business logic
Understand business requirements – Identify the business rules and processes.
Identify entities and responsibilities – Determine what objects/modules are involved.
Separate business logic from UI and database code – Business rules should not be tightly coupled with presentation or data-access code.
Design a service/business layer – Implement business rules in appropriate services or domain classes.
Apply OOP and SOLID principles – Maintain high cohesion and low coupling.
Handle validation and exceptions – Define proper rules for invalid or exceptional conditions.
Make the logic reusable – Avoid duplicate business rules in different parts of the application.
Test the business rules – Use unit and integration tests to verify correctness.
Document important rules – Document complex or critical business decisions for future maintenance.

I would not keep each function as a 600-line block. I would first refactor the application into smaller, modular, and reusable components.

Divide the system into logical modules/packages according to functionality.
Apply OOP and SOLID principles, especially the Single Responsibility Principle.
Break large functions into smaller methods and classes with well-defined responsibilities.
Use an appropriate Java framework such as Spring/Spring Boot to manage dependency injection, application layers, configuration, and integration.
Separate the application into layers such as Controller, Service, Repository, and Model.
Use design patterns where appropriate to improve reusability and maintainability.
Write unit, integration, and functional tests and automate them in the development pipeline.
Use Git, code review, documentation, and CI/CD to manage the large codebase.
